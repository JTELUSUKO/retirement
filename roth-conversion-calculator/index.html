What is a Roth conversion?

A Roth conversion moves money from a traditional IRA or pre-tax 401(k) into a Roth IRA. The amount you convert is added to your taxable income for the year, so you pay income tax on it now. In return, the money then grows tax-free, qualified withdrawals later are tax-free, and Roth IRAs have no required minimum distributions (RMDs) for the original owner.

Put simply, a conversion pays tax at today’s rate to avoid tax at a future rate. It tends to pay off when your rate now is lower than the rate you’d pay on the same money later.

How the tax on a conversion is figured

The converted amount is stacked on top of your other income for the year, so it is taxed at your highest (marginal) rates, not your average rate. A large conversion can span more than one bracket. On top of the bracket rate, a conversion can also:

  • Make more of your Social Security taxable. Up to 85% of benefits can be taxed as income rises, so each converted dollar can add more than a dollar of taxable income.
  • Shrink the senior deduction. For 2025–2028, people 65 and older get an extra deduction of up to $6,000 each, reduced by 6% of income above $75,000 (single) or $150,000 (married filing jointly).
  • Add state income tax, unless your state has no income tax or exempts retirement income at your age.

When converting can make sense

  • Low-income years, often between retiring and starting Social Security or RMDs, when your bracket may be lower than it will ever be again.
  • You expect a higher rate later, for example from large RMDs, a surviving spouse moving to single brackets, or tax rates rising.
  • You can pay the tax from other savings, so the full amount goes into the Roth.
  • You want to leave money to heirs, who generally must empty an inherited IRA within 10 years and may be in high brackets themselves.

When it may not

  • You expect a lower tax rate later than today.
  • You would pay the tax out of the conversion while under 59½: the withheld part counts as an early withdrawal and can owe a 10% penalty.
  • The extra income would push you into Medicare IRMAA surcharges or reduce health insurance marketplace (ACA) premium credits if you’re under 65.
  • You plan to leave the IRA to charity, which pays no income tax on it.

Medicare surcharges (IRMAA) and the two-year lookback

Medicare Part B and Part D premiums go up when your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) crosses certain levels, and they use your income from two years earlier. A conversion at 63 can raise your premiums at 65. Crossing a tier by even one dollar triggers the full surcharge for that tier, so it pays to know where the lines are.

Rules to know before converting

  • No income or amount limit. Anyone can convert any amount.
  • You can’t undo it. Conversions can no longer be recharacterized (reversed) since 2018.
  • It counts in the calendar year you convert. There is no April deadline as with contributions; a conversion on December 31 counts for that year.
  • Each conversion has its own five-year clock. If you are under 59½, withdrawing converted money within five years can owe the 10% penalty.
  • Plan for the tax bill. You may need to raise withholding or make estimated tax payments to avoid an underpayment penalty.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I convert each year?

A common approach is to “fill” a bracket: convert just enough to reach the top of your current bracket (often 12%, 22% or 24%) without spilling into the next one. The “Fill a bracket” table above shows those amounts for your numbers. Whether that’s right for you depends on your whole plan, so check it with a tax professional.

Is the tax rate on a conversion my bracket rate?

Not always. If you receive Social Security or are 65 or older, a conversion can also make more benefits taxable or shrink the senior deduction, so the real rate can be higher than your bracket. The calculator shows the actual extra tax.

Should I pay the tax from the conversion or from other money?

Paying from other savings usually works out better: the full converted amount keeps growing tax-free. Paying from the conversion shrinks what goes into the Roth, and before 59½ the withheld part can owe a 10% penalty.
